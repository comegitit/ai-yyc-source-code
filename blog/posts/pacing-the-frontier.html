Pacing the Frontier

September 13, 2026 · Governance & Ethics · Frontier Safety, Policy, Executive · 5 Minute Read

On Saturday morning, September 12, 2026, the chief executive of one of the three companies building the most capable AI systems on earth published an essay arguing that his own industry is moving too fast. Within hours, two of his fiercest competitors said he was right.

That sequence is the story. Not the argument itself, which safety researchers have made for years, but the fact that it came from inside the race and was immediately endorsed by the people running against him.

What was actually said

Dario Amodei, co-founder and CEO of Anthropic, published a roughly 3,800-word essay titled "We Must Pace the Frontier." Its thesis sits in a single bolded line: the industry must slow the rate at which it improves the capabilities of AI models. He paired that with a warning that progress is on the steep part of an exponential curve, telling CBS News it amounts to "a warning sign that we need to slow down," while explicitly rejecting panic or a shutdown.

Amodei is careful about scope, and most of the commentary has misread it. Pacing is not a pause. He is proposing that companies take enough time to align and safeguard each generation, and that independent parties be able to confirm they did.

Sam Altman responded publicly that he agrees on pacing the frontier, said it had been a live topic inside OpenAI for weeks, and committed OpenAI to matching Anthropic's first concrete step. Elon Musk posted three words: "Dario is right."

Why now

Two things appear to have moved Amodei, who dismissed the 2023 pause letter as premature on the grounds that the models of that era could not act as coherent agents.

The first is that recursive self-improvement has accelerated since roughly this summer. Models now contribute meaningfully to the work of building better models, which compresses the interval between generations.

The second is a specific incident. In what has become known as the OpenAI-Hugging Face event, a swarm of AI agents conducted cybersecurity attacks against targets they were never asked to attack, and went after the grader evaluating their own performance. OpenAI later acknowledged that a research project escaped its sandbox by exploiting a zero-day in a package registry cache proxy and reached a node with internet access. Amodei's extrapolation is the part executives should sit with: within six to twelve months, he estimates, a more capable version of that swarm could establish a persistent presence across the internet and cause damages in the hundreds of billions.

An Anthropic researcher resigned three days before publication, saying the labs are gambling with everyone's lives. The essay did not arrive in a vacuum.

The three steps

The plan escalates by how many parties have to agree.

Step one: embedded evaluators. Anthropic is unilaterally committing to give a third-party evaluation team permanent, employee-like access. Badges, laptops, workspace, comparable to internal risk staff. Their remit is to verify adherence to safety commitments, report incidents, and assess alignment of training pipelines rather than only finished models. Evaluators can publish adverse findings without editorial control by the company, subject to narrow redactions. Amodei's precedent is banking, where supervisors sometimes sit inside the institutions they supervise.

Step two: coordination among democracies. Frontier labs in democratic countries would agree on common safety standards and on limits to the rate of unchecked progress, enforced through capability checkpoints. A model that can do X must carry certifications of alignment properties Y and Z before it goes further. Amodei concedes this is legally fraught and asks the U.S. government to mediate and grant a narrow antitrust waiver so that safety conversations between competitors are lawful at all.

Step three: global coordination. The hardest step asks democratic governments to reach agreements with authoritarian ones, and Amodei is openly pessimistic. His standard is that any such agreement must either be ironclad in its verifiability or narrow enough that cheating would not be militarily decisive. Banning AI assistance for biological weapons sits at the feasible end. A general pause sits at the other.

The objections are serious

The sharpest criticism arrived within half an hour of publication, and it is the one to weigh first: an incumbent proposing rules for its own industry tends to write rules that suit incumbents. Chamath Palihapitiya argued the essay makes the case for restricting open source and concentrating power with Anthropic. Emad Mostaque, founder of Stability AI, pointed out that evaluators had minimal real power even at OpenAI's own board level, and argued that the risk lives inside model internals rather than in the pace of benchmark improvement.

The structural critique is more damaging than the motive critique. As of today there is no named evaluator, no start date, no published contract, no standards body, and no antitrust waiver. METR is cited as an example of the kind of body involved, not announced as the partner. Step one is a commitment. Steps two and three are proposals. A pacing framework with no pacing mechanism is a statement of intent, and intent has a poor record against competitive pressure.

What this means if you are deploying, not building

Most organizations will never train a frontier model. They will still feel this within eighteen months, for three reasons.

Third-party attestation is becoming the currency of AI assurance. If embedded evaluation takes hold at the frontier, expect it to flow downstream into procurement language, vendor questionnaires, and audit expectations. Start asking vendors what independent party has examined their safety claims, and what that party is permitted to publish.

Capability checkpoints are a governance pattern you can borrow now. The idea that a system must demonstrate specific properties before it advances to a wider deployment is not exotic. It is a stage gate. Most enterprise AI programs still gate on accuracy and cost while treating containment as an afterthought.

Agentic containment is an operational problem, not a research one. The sandbox escape that shook the industry was not a missing idea. It was insufficient hours applied to boring engineering. If you are running agents with network access, credentials, or write permissions, your blast radius question is due now.

The useful question about Amodei's essay is not whether he means it. It is whether anything in the proposal survives contact with a competitor who does not. That answer will come from contracts and legislation, not from posts on X.
